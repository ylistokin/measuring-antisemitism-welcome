Dear Readers:

Welcome to “Measuring Antisemitism”, the [Brodie Center for Jewish and Israeli Law](https://law.yale.edu/centers-and-workshops/brodie-center-jewish-and-israeli-law) at Yale Law School’s new blog on empirical studies of antisemitism.

Like [many](https://2021-2025.state.gov/u-s-national-statement-for-the-osce-conference-on-addressing-antisemitism-in-the-osce-region/?safe=1) [others](https://commission.europa.eu/strategy-and-policy/policies/justice-and-fundamental-rights/combatting-discrimination/racism-and-xenophobia/combating-antisemitism/eu-strategy-combating-antisemitism-and-fostering-jewish-life-2021-2030/first-progress-report-eu-strategy-combating_en), we think antisemitism is an important and growing social problem. To do something about antisemitism, we need to understand it. And we believe that the empirical study of antisemitism offers a crucial but underdeveloped means of understanding the problem.

At Measuring Antisemitism, we try to support our assertions with data. So why do we think antisemitism is a significant and growing problem? Consider Figure 1, which shows the FBI’s hate crime data for the US, counting “crimes that are reported to the police and judged by the police to be motivated by bias towards a certain person or group of people because of characteristics about them such as race, sexual orientation, or religion.”[^1] Hate crimes are the “tip-of-the-iceberg” of antisemitism and other types of bias. As the paradigmatic example of group-based animus, rates of hate crimes give us an objective measure of the worst manifestations of hate.

Figure 1 graphs the number of annual antisemitic hate crimes per 100,000 Jewish Americans in the FBI hate crime data. [^2] For frame of reference, Figure 1 also shows the annual number of anti-Black hate crimes per 100,000 Black Americans from the FBI and census data. The data shows that Jewish Americans suffered from approximately 1.5-3 times more hate crimes per capita than Black Americans during the period 1991-2021. Measured by hate crimes, antisemitism has been a significant American problem for decades. And the problem is growing. Between 2021 and 2024, the antisemitic hate crime rate more than doubled. In 2024, Jews suffered from hate crimes at almost 4 times the rate of Black Americans. Even in absolute terms, the 1,973 antisemitic hate crimes in 2024 are over 60% of the 3,171 anti-Black hate crimes that year, remarkable because the Black American population is more than six times larger than the Jewish population.

While data sources like Figure 1 suggest that antisemitism is (on some dimensions) a more acute problem than anti-Black racism, empirical scholarship on anti-Black racism dwarfs empirical scholarship on antisemitism. When ChatGPT conducts a census of papers published between 2000-2026 in the “top 10” journals of the fields of economics, political science, psychology, and sociology and enumerates the number of empirical papers with a “primary focus” on modern (post-1960) anti-Black racism and the number of empirical papers with a “primary focus” on modern (post-1960) anti-Semitism, it finds more than 15 papers on anti-Black racism for each paper on antisemitism.[^3] Figure 2 charts the results by field. Sociology and Psychology show the biggest tilt towards anti-Black racism, while Political Science devotes relatively more attention to antisemitism.

![Figure 2: Modern empirical papers in top-10 journals, by field](figure2/outputs/figure_2_modern_top_10_journals.png)

Even though Jewish Americans suffer from hate crimes at a much higher rate than Black Americans, academics produce much more empirical scholarship per capita about anti-Black racism than antisemitism. While there are many potential explanations for the disparity, including the differing correlation between socioeconomic status and Jewish and black identity, Figure 2 supports the claim that empirical study of antisemitism is underdeveloped—at least relative to the empirical academic study of anti-Black racism.

Hate crimes, of course, offer only one measure of the relative salience of antisemitism. Unlike hate crimes, measures of explicit warmth/animus, such as the [American National Elections Survey’s](https://electionstudies.org/) (ANES) “feelings thermometers”, [^4] do not indicate extraordinarily levels of US antisemitism. [^5] Instead, Americans in the ANES report relatively warm feelings towards Jews relative to White Americans, Asians, and Muslim Americans. Interestingly, ANES respondents report positive feelings towards Black Americans that resemble their attitudes towards Jewish Americans. Unlike the hate crime data, the ANES data and some other measures of explicit animus offer no reason for empirical studies of contemporary anti-Black racism or antisemitism to diverge from their shares of the population.

But measures of explicit favorability/animus such as feelings thermometers can be misleading. Social desirability concerns may diminish the willingness of respondents to candidly acknowledge negative feelings towards outgroups.[^6] To address these concerns, lets explore a very different tool for measuring relations between groups-- “implicit bias” scores. Instead of asking respondents explicitly about their biases, the Implicit Association Test (IAT) measures implicit associations between minority “out” groups and positive and negative valences, such as the strength of the association of “Black” people with the adjectives “good” and “bad”. The strength of association is measured through reaction times. Survey respondents sort words or images using the same two keys. If someone responds faster when “Black” and “bad” share a key than when “White” and “bad” share a key, then the test interprets that difference as a stronger automatic association between Black and bad. The strength of these associations is measured by the IAT’s “D” Score. Positive D scores indicate an association between the “out” group and “bad”. The higher the D score, the stronger the association between the group in question and “bad”.

The IAT has been rapidly adopted in the social scientific study of race. A 2021 literature review found over 4,000 published papers using the IAT, which was only developed around 2000.[^7] Project Implicit at Harvard University invites online respondents to take the Implicit Association Test for several different “in” and “out” group combinations, including Black/White and Jewish/Christian. To my knowledge, however, antisemitism scholars have not used Project Implicit’s Jewish/Christian data.

Figure 3 presents mean Black/White and Jewish/Christian IAT D Scores and 95% confidence intervals from Project Implicit surveys between 2023-2025. Since IAT respondents are known to favor their own group over others,[^8] the Figure 3 plots exclude Black respondents for the Black curve and Jewish respondents for the Jewish curve.

![Figure 3: Implicit Association Test D scores for Blacks and Jews, 2023–2025](figure3/outputs/figure3_iat_black_jewish_ci.png)

*Data and code: [figure3/](figure3/)*

Even though the Implicit Association Test measures a very different manifestation of bias than hate crimes per capita, Figure 3 broadly resembles Figure 1. IAT D scores for Blacks and Jews indicate comparable levels of implicit bias in 2023. Between late 2023 and late 2025, however, implicit bias against Jews increases markedly (by more than 40%). This increase echoes the dramatic increase in hate crimes against Jews between 2023 and 2025 depicted in Figure 1. Black D scores also increase from 2023-2025, but modestly. As a result, by the end of 2025, the mean Project Implicit respondent more strongly associates Jewish with bad than Black with bad.

Two very different measures of bias (hate crimes per capita, Figure 1, and implicit bias, Figure 3) support the claim that antisemitism is an important and growing social problem. On some dimensions, modern antisemitism is “worse” than anti-Black racism. Yet Figure 2 provides evidence that antisemitism is understudied by academics relative to anti-Black racism.

The goal of this analysis is not to direct scholarly attention away from the study of anti-Black racism. Indeed, one of inspirations for this substack is the extraordinary body of empirical work on anti-Black racism across the social sciences that is reflected in Figure 2. My co-author Yehonatan Givati and I think this work changed minds, deepened understanding of anti-Black racism, and meaningfully affected policy. In conjunction with other efforts,[^9] we hope Measuring Antisemitism helps catalyze a similar revolution in the empirical study of antisemitism.

Yehonatan and I hope that Measuring Antisemitism: 1. Provides a forum for lively empirical scholarship and debate about antisemitism. Towards this end, we welcome original submissions that are shorter or more informal than journal articles and need a home. This invitation very much includes well-constructed “negative results” that find no evidence for a particular hypothesis about antisemitism. 2. Provide a space for researchers to learn about developments in the field, which spans the social sciences and humanities. We therefore invite summaries of papers published in professional journals. 3. Provide a hub for making datasets on antisemitism available to researchers, which are currently hard to locate (at least in our experience). 4. Become a place for academics and others interested in understanding antisemitism can interact. We aim to make all of our entries accessible to interested laypeople and invite submissions by non-professionals that criticize academic work, identify holes in the available data, and engage in informal but sophisticated statistical analysis themselves.

We invite you to join us on this intellectual journey.
